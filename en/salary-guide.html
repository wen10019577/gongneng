Why Does Take-Home Pay Start Shrinking Mid-Year? The Cumulative Withholding Method, Worked Out

The gross salary on your payslip hasn't changed by a cent, yet from some month onward your take-home pay is a few hundred yuan less. That usually isn't your employer deducting wrongly — it's the cumulative withholding method reaching a 'bracket jump' month. This guide works through a complete example on a monthly salary of 20,000 yuan, month by month to the end of the year, so you can predict for yourself which months your take-home pay will change and how much tax you'll pay in total for the year.

Written by Ernzi; worked examples re-checked · Updated: 2026-11-04

The example in this guide is a constructed fixed-salary scenario: employed at the same employer all year, with unchanged monthly salary and deductions and no year-end bonus. The tax rate table and calculation method follow the withholding rate table for comprehensive income under China's Individual Income Tax Law and the State Taxation Administration's explanation of the cumulative withholding method; your actual result will be affected by your local social insurance contribution base, special additional deductions and income structure, so go by your payslip and the annual reconciliation. This page does not provide individual tax advice; for complex situations, consult the tax authorities or a professional.

First, the method: cumulative withholding in three steps

  1. Calculate cumulative taxable income: add up income from January to the current month, then subtract the cumulative basic deduction (5,000 yuan per month), cumulative special deductions (social insurance and housing fund contributions you pay personally), and cumulative special additional deductions (children’s education, support for elderly parents, housing rent or mortgage interest, etc.).
  2. Apply the annual rate table to get cumulative tax: put the cumulative taxable income into the withholding rate table for comprehensive income (seven brackets from 3% to 45%) and use “cumulative amount × tax rate − quick deduction” to get the total tax that should have been withheld up to the current month.
  3. Subtract what was already withheld — that’s this month’s withholding: cumulative tax payable minus the amounts withheld in previous months; the difference is the individual income tax withheld from this month’s salary.

The key is step two: the rate table is looked up by the cumulative amount. Early in the year the cumulative amount is small and sits in the low 3% bracket; as the months go by, the cumulative amount crosses the 36000-yuan, 144000-yuan and other thresholds one after another, the marginal rate jumps to 10%, then 20%, and that month’s withholding rises accordingly. Take-home pay shrinking doesn’t mean any month was over-deducted — it means the low-rate allowance was used up first, early in the year.

Complete worked example: 20,000 yuan a month, a month-by-month table for the whole year

Suppose the monthly gross salary is 20000 yuan, personal social insurance and housing fund contributions total 2500 yuan, and special additional deductions are 2000 yuan (for example a shared deduction for supporting elderly parents, or housing rent — replace with what you actually claim). Then each month’s new taxable income = 20000 − 5000 − 2500 − 2000 = 10500 yuan.

The reason: in April the cumulative amount of 42000 yuan crosses the 36000-yuan threshold for the first time. Of that month’s new 10500 yuan, part is still taxed at 3% and the part above the threshold at 10%, so April’s withholding of 735 yuan is a transitional figure; from May onward, each month’s new income falls entirely in the 10% bracket and withholding settles at 10500 × 10% = 1050 yuan. Total individual income tax for the year is 10080 yuan, and total take-home pay for the year is 199920 yuan (not counting a year-end bonus).

For a feel of the jump point at another level: with a monthly salary of 15000 yuan, social insurance and housing fund of 1800 yuan and special additional deductions of 1000 yuan, each month’s new taxable income is 7200 yuan; the first 5 months cumulate to exactly 36000 yuan, with 216 yuan withheld each month; from month 6 it jumps into the 10% bracket and monthly withholding becomes 720 yuan. The higher the income, the earlier the jump comes; people whose monthly new taxable income exceeds 12000 yuan will jump again into the 20% bracket in the second half of the year.

Three common misunderstandings

1. Less take-home pay ≠ more tax over the year. Cumulative withholding simply spreads the year’s tax across the months by progress; a mid-year bracket jump is only a matter of pacing. The tax computed by applying the rate table once to the full year’s taxable income equals the sum of the monthly withholdings in a fixed-salary scenario (10080 yuan in both cases in the example above). What actually changes your annual tax is the income and deductions themselves, not the bracket jump.

2. Dividing the annual tax by 12 to estimate monthly withholding will never match. A common mistake is to average the annual tax over the months and then question why a particular month withheld more. In this guide’s example the average is 840 yuan per month, but no single month actually withholds 840 yuan — lower first, higher later is the normal shape of cumulative withholding.

3. If you file special additional deductions late and only catch up mid-year, take-home pay suddenly rises for one month. After the late filing, cumulative deductions increase all at once, cumulative tax payable falls, and that month’s withholding drops noticeably or even to zero (any excess withheld can’t be refunded as a negative amount that month — it waits for the annual reconciliation). This is also normal; there’s no need to worry that something was calculated wrong.

Situations where this model doesn’t apply

  • Starting, leaving or changing jobs mid-year: a new employer generally restarts the accumulation from your first month there, and the annual reconciliation merges everything, so withholding and the reconciliation can differ considerably.
  • A year-end bonus: the bonus can be taxed separately or merged into comprehensive income, and the two methods give different results — see the FAQ below; this site’s comprehensive income annual tax tool can compare at the full-year level.
  • Income from multiple sources: if you also have remuneration for services, author’s remuneration, royalties or salaries from two employers, each withholding agent calculates its own part and everything must be merged at the annual reconciliation.
  • Mid-year social insurance base adjustments: many places publish a new social insurance contribution base mid-year and settle the difference, so a sudden change in one month’s social insurance deduction has nothing to do with the income tax bracket jump — look at the two lines on the payslip separately.

These situations don’t mean the tool calculated wrong; they mean the fixed-salary model’s assumptions don’t hold. Working out which situation you’re in matters more than agonising over one month’s figure.

FAQ

My salary hasn’t gone up or down — why does my take-home pay get smaller?

Because salary income tax is withheld by the cumulative withholding method: once cumulative taxable income crosses thresholds such as 36000 yuan mid-year, the applicable rate jumps from 3% to 10% or higher, that month’s withholding increases and take-home pay falls. It isn’t a deduction error; at the annual reconciliation everything is settled uniformly against actual full-year income.

What is the 5,000-yuan monthly deduction about?

The basic deduction for comprehensive income is 60000 yuan per year, accumulated at 5000 yuan per month during withholding. On top of that, the social insurance and housing fund contributions you pay personally (special deductions) and special additional deductions such as children’s education and housing rent make up the part that is subtracted from income first each month.

If I change jobs mid-year, will the change in take-home pay be even bigger?

Yes. A new employer usually restarts the accumulation from the month you join and doesn’t carry over the previous employer’s cumulative figures, so the rate is on the low side in the first months; at the annual reconciliation, income from both employers is merged and you may owe additional tax. Situations such as two jobs or remuneration for services are more complex — go by the annual reconciliation result.

Can tax that was over-withheld be refunded?

Yes. File the annual reconciliation of comprehensive income between March 1 and June 30 of the following year; if your actual annual tax payable is less than the tax already withheld, the difference is refunded — overpayments refunded, shortfalls topped up. The reconciliation also involves choices such as how the year-end bonus is taxed, so go by the tax authorities’ system calculation.

Which is better for a year-end bonus: taxed separately or merged into comprehensive income?

There’s no universal answer; it depends on which bracket your annual taxable income falls into. If your income is lower and your comprehensive income bracket is below the bracket the bonus would get under separate taxation, merging may work out better; at higher incomes, separate taxation may save more. The period for which the policy applies follows the official documents of the year; at reconciliation time you can usually trial-calculate both ways and then choose.
