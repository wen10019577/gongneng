Is the Bigger Pack Always Better Value? Convert Everything to a Price per 100 g

Different pack sizes cannot be compared by total price directly; and once everything is converted to a price per 100 g, the option with a unit price 12% lower is still not necessarily the one to buy. This guide sets out the formula, three comparisons and three preconditions, all in one place.

Written by Ernzi; worked examples re-checked · Updated: 2026-11-04

The figures in this article are teaching examples constructed by this site and do not represent any merchant's promotion or a real user's bill. You can re-check each step and then substitute your own data.

The formula: first make the denominators use the same unit

Unit price = amount actually paid including shipping ÷ (net content per pack × number of packs), then multiplied by 100 and shown as the price per 100 g (or per 100 ml).

Three points are easiest to get wrong. First, the price must be the final amount paid: a unit price that leaves out the discounts or the shipping is fictitious — for how discounts are calculated, see threshold discount or percentage off first. Second, for a multipack the total quantity must be multiplied by the number of packs; do not compare one pack’s specification against the other side’s whole bundle. Third, the units must match: enter kilograms on one side and grams on the other and the conclusion is void. Mass is compared in grams, volume in millilitres, and identical discrete items in pieces — the three denominators cannot be converted into one another.

Comparison one: dry goods — unit price 12% lower, total 31 yuan higher

PackSpecificationPaid incl. shippingPer 100 g
A500 g × 1 bag18.9 yuan3.78 yuan
B750 g × 2 bags (1500 g in total)49.9 yuanAbout 3.33 yuan

Re-check: A is 18.9 ÷ 500 × 100 = 3.78 yuan/100 g; B is 49.9 ÷ 1500 × 100 ≈ 3.3267 yuan/100 g, so B’s unit price is about 11.99% lower. But buying a set of B costs 49.9 yuan today, 31 yuan more than one bag of A. If you can finish 1500 g, the right comparison is three bags of A (56.7 yuan) — against that, B really does save 6.8 yuan. If you actually need only 500 g and the rest would sit until it expires, the extra 31 yuan buys waste, and no unit price, however low, makes that good value. A cheaper unit price, spending less this time, and being able to finish it are three different things; judge them separately.

Comparison two: rice — unit prices only 9% apart, so it depends on how fast you eat

PackSpecificationPricePer 100 gPer kg
A5 kg32.9 yuan0.658 yuan6.58 yuan
B10 kg59.9 yuan0.599 yuan5.99 yuan

B is 0.59 yuan cheaper per kilogram, saving 5.9 yuan a bag. But once opened, rice can suffer insects, moisture and declining taste: living alone and cooking infrequently, 10 kg can take over half a year to finish, and the 5.9 yuan saved is easily eaten back by spoilage; a family of four finishing it in a month makes B an easy choice. The order of judgement is: first estimate ‘how long until it is finished’ from your household’s consumption rate, then weigh that against shelf life and storage conditions, and only then look at the unit-price difference. When the unit price differs by less than 5%, spoilage and tied-up cash usually deserve more attention than the unit price.

Comparison three: things not sold by the gram need a different denominator

Tissues are compared per 100 sheets. A: 100 sheets × 10 packs = 1000 sheets, 19.9 yuan, 1.99 yuan per 100 sheets; B: 120 sheets × 12 packs = 1440 sheets, 27.9 yuan, about 1.94 yuan per 100 sheets. A difference of only about 2.6% — at that point ply, sheet size and strength matter more than the unit price; do not order on the number alone.

Shampoo is compared per 100 ml. A: 500 ml for 29.9 yuan, 5.98 yuan per 100 ml; B: 880 ml for 45.9 yuan, about 5.22 yuan per 100 ml, about 13% lower. Liquids keep well and usage is steady, so for this kind of product the bulk pack’s unit-price advantage usually really pays out.

Cases where weight cannot be compared directly: cleaning products of different concentrations should be converted to a cost per use; food with bone, shell or ice glaze should be estimated by its edible portion; fresh and dried goods have completely different water contents. First unify the denominator to ‘the amount you can actually use’, then apply the formula.

Four details when entering the numbers

  1. Finish the discount and shipping calculation first, then put the final payment into the price field; if the shipping is not settled yet, see how to compare padding an order for free shipping.
  2. For a multipack, enter the price of the whole set once and the pack count as the number of packs in the set, so the price is not multiplied twice.
  3. Use the net content for the specification, not the gross weight of the packaging; for ‘extra quantity, same price’ offers, count the bonus quantity into the net content before calculating.
  4. A free gift in exactly the same specification as the main product, which you will use up, may be counted into the quantity; gifts that differ or that you will not use should be recorded separately — do not let them dilute the unit price.

This site’s tools compare on unrounded results and display four decimal places. When the two unit prices differ by less than 1%, the practical difference is negligible — hand the choice back to shelf life, taste and storage space.

FAQ

How is a unit price calculated fairly?

Unit price = amount actually paid including shipping ÷ (net content per pack × number of packs). The price must be the final payment (discounts and shipping both included), the content must be the total net content, and both sides must use the same unit: grams for solids, millilitres for liquids, pieces or sheets for identical discrete items.

Why does a lower unit price not always mean buying the bigger pack?

A lower unit price only says each unit is cheaper; the total outlay is still higher, and whatever you cannot finish is waste. For food, look at shelf life and usage rate; for household goods, look at storage space. First estimate how much you can use up within the shelf life, then decide the pack size.

Can free gifts count toward the net content?

A gift in exactly the same specification as the main product, which you will definitely use up, may be counted into the total; a gift of a different specification or one you will not use should be recorded separately — do not spread it into the unit price, or the unit price comes out artificially low.

Which products cannot be compared by price per 100 g alone?

Cleaners of different concentrations should be compared by cost per use; food with bone, shell or ice glaze by edible portion; tissues by the sheet (also considering ply and size); and products whose water content differs greatly cannot be compared by weight directly either. First unify everything to ‘the amount you can actually use’, then compare prices.
