How Many Days of Annual Leave Do You Get? The Three Service Tiers of 5/10/15 Days and New-Hire Pro-Rating

"I've only been at this company two years, why do I get 5 days of annual leave?" "The colleague next to me with 11 years of service gets 10 days" — the first rule of annual leave (under China's labour rules) is counter-intuitive: it depends on your <strong>cumulative years of work</strong>, not on how long you've been at this company. This guide explains the three tiers of days, new-hire pro-rating, the situations where you don't qualify, and unused-leave pay, all at once.

Written by Ernzi; worked examples re-checked · Updated: 2026-11-04

This guide is compiled from the Regulations on Paid Annual Leave for Employees and the Implementation Measures for Paid Annual Leave for Enterprise Employees (China). Government bodies, public institutions, state-owned enterprises and local detailed rules may differ, and your own employer's attendance and leave system takes precedence; disputes are governed by the reading of the HR department and labour arbitration. This page does not provide legal advice for individual cases.

The three tiers: work out your cumulative years of service first

Cumulative years of workAnnual leave days
1 year or more, less than 10 years5 days
10 years or more, less than 20 years10 days
20 years or more15 days

“Cumulative” includes all periods worked at different employers, generally proven by social insurance contribution records, labour contracts, personnel files and the like. With less than 1 cumulative year, there is no statutory annual leave that year (company welfare leave is a separate matter). So if you change jobs after a full year of work, the new employer should also grant leave by your total years of service rather than starting from zero — have your previous employer’s social insurance records ready when you join, and you’ll save a lot of explaining. Use this site’s date-interval tool to work out the start and end of your own cumulative service first.

Your first year at a new employer: pro-rated by the days remaining

If you already qualify but have newly joined an employer, that year’s annual leave is pro-rated by formula:

Annual leave for the year = (calendar days remaining after joining ÷ 365) × full-year entitlement, with any part of less than 1 day after pro-rating disregarded.

Example: Xiao Chen has 6 cumulative years of work (entitlement 5 days) and joins a new company on July 1. From July 1 to December 31 of that year there are 184 days; 184 ÷ 365 × 5 = 2.52 days; the fraction of less than 1 day is dropped, so at the new employer he can take 2 days that year. Note that the pro-rating applies only to “the first year at a new employer”; from the second year on, the full-year entitlement applies. The same applies when leaving a job: that year’s leave is pro-rated by the days worked — leave over-taken is generally not clawed back from wages, and leave not taken is paid as unused-leave pay on the pro-rated number of days.

Four situations where you don’t get that year’s annual leave

  1. You are entitled to winter/summer vacations by law and the days taken exceed the annual leave days (e.g. school teaching staff).
  2. You took 20 days or more of personal leave in total, and the employer did not deduct wages as prescribed.
  3. Sick leave over the limit: with cumulative service of 1 year or more and less than 10 years, sick leave totalling 2 months or more; with 10 years or more and less than 20 years, sick leave totalling 3 months or more; with 20 years or more, sick leave totalling 4 months or more.
  4. After taking annual leave in a year, one of the above sick-leave situations arises in the same year — you don’t get the following year’s annual leave.

Of these, sick leave is the easiest to overlook: coming back from a long sick leave to find no annual leave is usually not an HR miscalculation but this threshold being triggered. If you need to plan ahead, use the service and age tool to confirm which tier your years of service fall into first.

The unused-leave pay rules

Where the employer genuinely cannot schedule the leave because of work needs, with the employee’s own consent it may be carried over 1 year; where it truly cannot be scheduled, unused annual leave pay is paid at 300% of the employee’s daily wage income. That 300% includes the 100% already paid for normal attendance, so the actual additional amount is 200%. Conversely, where the employee declines the leave for personal reasons, the employer may pay only the normal wage — the premise of “either take the leave, or get the extra 200%” is that the reason the leave went untaken lies with the employer. Keep the communication records before negotiating compensation.

How to prove cumulative service: bring these three things when you join

The word “cumulative” rests, in practice, on evidence. When changing jobs, have ready: your social insurance contribution records over the years (downloadable from the local human-resources-and-social-security app of the place where you were insured), the labour contract or separation certificate from your previous employer, and proof of personnel-file custody (if any). Example: Xiao Wang worked 3 years at Company A and 4 years at Company B; joining Company C he has 7 cumulative years, and in that year (if a full year) he is entitled to 5 days; after 3 more years he reaches 10 cumulative years and moves up into the 10-day tier. If social insurance contributions have gap months, whether the corresponding periods count toward cumulative service is read differently in different localities; the common handling is to accumulate by the periods of work actually on record, and in disputes the determination of the human resources and social security department governs. Hand the materials into your personnel file at onboarding registration — far more reliable than rummaging through boxes just before taking leave. Most “annual leave under-counted” disputes are lost not on the rules but on the evidence.

FAQ

Is annual leave based on service at this employer or cumulative service?

On cumulative years of work, including periods at other employers: 5 days at 1 year, 10 days at 10 years, 15 days at 20 years. Changing jobs doesn’t reset it to zero, as proven by social insurance records and the like.

How is annual leave pro-rated in the first year at a new employer?

By (calendar days remaining at this employer ÷ 365) × full-year entitlement, with the fraction of less than 1 day disregarded. Joining on July 1 with a 5-day entitlement pro-rates to 2 days.

What happens if I don’t use up my annual leave?

With your consent it can be carried over 1 year; if it truly can’t be taken, it is paid at 300% of the daily wage (an actual extra 200%). If you decline it yourself, the employer may pay only the normal wage. On leaving a job it is handled by pro-rating over your days in post.
